Learn the basics

Every term StockIQ uses, explained with a small example. If a page ever shows a number you do not recognise, it is defined here.

Size and sales

Market Cap

The price of one share multiplied by the number of shares. It is what the market says the whole company is worth today.

Example: 100 crore shares at Rs 500 each gives a market cap of Rs 50,000 crore.

Revenue

Total money customers paid the company, before any costs are subtracted. Also called sales or turnover.

Watch for: revenue growing while profit falls means costs are rising faster than sales.

EBITDA

Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation. It shows what the core operations earn before financing decisions and accounting choices.

Example: revenue Rs 1,000 crore, operating costs Rs 800 crore, so EBITDA is Rs 200 crore and the EBITDA margin is 20%.

PAT (Profit After Tax)

Also called net profit. What is left after every cost, interest payment and tax. This is the number that belongs to shareholders.

EPS (Earnings Per Share)

Net profit divided by the number of shares. Your slice of the profit for each share you own.

Example: profit Rs 500 crore, 100 crore shares, so EPS is Rs 5.

How expensive is it?

P/E Ratio

Price divided by earnings per share.

Example: a company earns Rs 10 per share and its stock costs Rs 200. Its P/E is 20. That means investors are paying Rs 20 for every Rs 1 of annual earnings.

A high P/E is not automatically bad and a low one is not automatically a bargain. A high P/E usually means buyers expect strong growth; a low one often means they expect trouble. The question is always whether they are right.

P/B Ratio

Price divided by book value per share, where book value is assets minus liabilities. Below 1 means you pay less than the accounting value of the company. Most useful for banks and asset-heavy businesses.

EV/EBITDA

Enterprise Value divided by EBITDA. Enterprise value is market cap plus debt minus cash: what it would really cost to buy the whole business. It lets you compare two companies fairly even when one carries much more debt than the other.

PEG Ratio

P/E divided by the earnings growth rate. The old rule of thumb is that a PEG near 1 means growth roughly justifies the price. It is a rough guide, not a rule.

Dividend Yield

The yearly dividend as a percentage of the share price.

Example: a Rs 10 dividend on a Rs 500 share is a 2% yield: Rs 2 a year for every Rs 100 invested.

Is it a good business?

ROE (Return on Equity)

Profit as a percentage of the owners money in the business.

Example: ROE of 20% means that for every Rs 100 of shareholder capital, the company earned Rs 20 of profit in a year.

A company can raise its ROE simply by borrowing more, so always read ROE next to debt-to-equity.

ROCE (Return on Capital Employed)

Like ROE, but it counts borrowed money as well as the owners money. Because you cannot flatter it with debt, it usually says more about the quality of the underlying business.

Debt / Equity

Borrowed money divided by the owners money. 0 means debt-free. Above 1 means the company has borrowed more than the owners put in.

Debt is not automatically bad. It magnifies both good years and bad ones, which is exactly why it matters.

Free Cash Flow

Cash left after paying to run and grow the business (operating cash flow minus capital spending).

Profit is an accounting number and involves judgement. Free cash flow is actual money. When the two drift apart for several years, that is worth investigating.

Reading the price chart

Moving Average

The average closing price over the last N days, recalculated each day. A 200-day moving average smooths out daily noise so a longer trend is visible.

Price above its 200 DMA is often described as a long-term uptrend. That describes the past, not the future.

RSI

Relative Strength Index. Compares the size of recent gains with recent losses on a 0 to 100 scale. Above 70 is called overbought, below 30 oversold.

These labels describe how one-sided recent trading has been. A stock can stay above 70 for months.

Support and Resistance

Support is a price area where buyers have stepped in before. Resistance is where sellers have. They are patterns in past trading, not rules the market must obey.

MACD and ADX

MACD compares a short-term and a medium-term moving average, showing whether momentum is turning. ADX measures how strong a trend is, not which way it points: below 20 means price is drifting sideways.

Where StockIQ gets its data

The app is set up so no single data source is baked into the code. Every source implements the same seven methods, so swapping one for another is a single line in the .env file.

Demo mode (the default)

With DATA_PROVIDER=demo, every price, statement and headline is generated sample data. It is clearly labelled DEMO DATA everywhere it appears. It exists so you can see and test every screen without paying for market data. None of it is real.

Switching to real data

Set DATA_PROVIDER=yfinance in .env and run pip install yfinance. That gives real prices, historical candles and annual financials. Note that yfinance reads Yahoo Finance's public endpoints: it is not an official NSE or BSE feed and is not licensed for redistribution. For anything beyond personal research, use a licensed vendor or an official exchange feed.

Adding your own source

Copy services/providers/demo_provider.py, keep the method names and the returned dictionary keys identical, and register your class in services/providers/__init__.py. The README has a worked example.

What StockIQ deliberately will not do

  • It never prints BUY, SELL or HOLD. It shows the conditions that would make a stock more attractive, the conditions that argue for waiting, and the ones that should make you cautious.
  • It never predicts a share price and never sets a price target.
  • It never fills a gap with a plausible-looking number. If a figure is not available it says so.
  • The AI is only allowed to explain numbers this app already computed. It is instructed not to state any figure that is not in that data.
  • It is not a SEBI-registered investment adviser and does not give personalised investment advice.
